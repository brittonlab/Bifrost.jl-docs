Deriving the Generators

BIFROST tries to solve the differential equation

\[\frac{\partial J(z)}{\partial z} = K(z) J(z)\]

where $J(z)$ is the Jones matrix for our system (which also depends on e.g. $\omega$, the angular frequency of the light), $z$ is the position coordinate along the fiber, and $K(z)$ is a "generator." The generator contains all of the fundamental physics in the problem, so this document shows how to derive it.

Our formulation is very similar to that of Przhiyalkovsky et al., "Polarization Dynamics of Light Propagating in Bent Spun Birefringent Fiber," Journal of Lightwave Technology 38(24), 6879-6885 (2020), DOI: 10.1109/jlt.2020.3017795.

Formulating the Equation

Suppose our fiber is laid along the $z$-axis. Propagation of monochromatic light in the fiber is dictated by the wave equation

\[\frac{\partial^2\vec{E}}{\partial z^2} = -k_0^2 \epsilon \vec{E}.\]

Here $k_0 = \omega/c$ and $\epsilon$ is the permittivity tensor. This is what contains all of the interesting physics; we'll come back to it in a moment.

We approximate the waves as transverse, so $\vec{E} = (E_x, E_y)$ (i.e. only two components). Time dependence is $e^{-i\omega t}$ (the convention stated in Bifrost.PolarizationMath), so a forward wave advances as $e^{+ikz}$. There is a common phase gained by both polarization modes of the form $e^{+i k_0 \bar{n} z}$ where $\bar{n}$ is an average refractive index. Let's transform this away by defining $E_{x,y} = \mathcal{E}_{x,y} e^{+ik_0 \bar{n}z}$. If the anisotropy is low ($\epsilon_i, \epsilon_c \ll \bar{\epsilon}$), then this acts like a slowly-varying envelope approximation and, upon substitution into our wave equation, we can ignore the second-order derivatives of $\mathcal{E}_{x,y}$, which turns our wave equation into a Schrödinger-like equation:

\[2i\bar{n} \frac{\partial \mathcal{E}}{\partial z} = -k_0(\epsilon - \bar{n}^2)\mathcal{E}.\]

So the "generator" we're interested in is

\[K(z) = +\frac{i}{2} \frac{k_0}{\bar{n}} (\epsilon - \bar{n}^2).\]

The Permittivity Tensor

The permittivity tensor has contributions from every birefringence mechanism available. It explicitly describes how the two modes gain phase, so we can use our physical intuition to write it down. We must pick a basis for doing so: BIFROST uses the parallel-transported (Bishop) pair $(e_1, e_2)$ — zero twist about the tangent, continuous along the whole path — which is the gauge in which free propagation contributes no frame term at all (see The transverse frame and the birefringence gauge). On a straight fiber, where the distinction is invisible, we can write as a starting point

\[\epsilon = \bar{\epsilon} + i\epsilon_c \left( \begin{array}{cc} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{array} \right) + \epsilon_i \left( \begin{array}{cc} \cos 2\xi z & \sin 2\xi z \\ \sin 2\xi z & -\cos 2\xi z \end{array} \right).\]

Here $\bar{\epsilon} = \bar{n}^2$ is the average permittivity, $\epsilon_c$ is the circular birefringence, and $\epsilon_i$ is the intrinsic linear birefringence, which is rotating with the spin of the fiber at spin rate $\xi$. Writing $\xi = 2\pi/L_s$ with a single spin pitch $L_s$ is the constant-rate special case used here for clarity. In general the spin rate is an arbitrary function of arc length, $\xi = \xi(z)$, and the implementation treats it that way: the rate of a spin run, authored with spin!(pb; rate), is nothing, a constant, or a Function of run-local material arc length, and the integrator (_integrate_rate) takes the analytic branch for a constant rate and adaptive Gauss–Kronrod quadrature for a function rate. Replace $2\xi z$ below with $2\int_0^z \xi(z')\,dz'$ for the function-valued case.

The remaining freedom in the basis is a single constant rotation — the anchor $e_1(0)$, fixed by a static lab-frame convention (see the frame page, Section 6). We mostly don't care about this overall rotation because we send in light of all polarizations and the observables (DGD in particular) are gauge-invariant.

With bending, the linear birefringence direction of the bend is the projection of the curvature vector onto $(e_1, e_2)$: $\theta_b = \operatorname{atan2}(\vec k\cdot e_2,\ \vec k\cdot e_1)$, while the intrinsic (ellipticity/stress) axes sit at their frozen angle plus the accumulated spin and twist phases. No geometric quantity ever rotates the basis itself — the bookkeeping that the Frenet–Serret basis would require (and that historically produced the issue #88/#89 family of bugs) does not arise.

If there were no spin, then we would say

\[\epsilon = \bar{\epsilon} + i\epsilon_c \left( \begin{array}{cc} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{array} \right) + \epsilon_i \left( \begin{array}{cc} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{array} \right).\]

The gyrotropic term enters $K$ as

\[K_c = \frac{k_0 \epsilon_c}{2\bar{n}} \left( \begin{array}{cc} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{array} \right),\]

which is circular_birefringence_generator(Δβc) with $\Delta\beta_c = k_0\epsilon_c/\bar{n}$. Its eigenstate $[1, i]$, left-circular in this convention, has the higher index when $\epsilon_c > 0$, consistent with $\Delta\beta_c = \beta_{LC} - \beta_{RC}$.

Assembling the Fiber Generator

The implementation assembles the generator from independently-modeled mechanisms. From here on we write the propagation coordinate as the arc length $s$ along the centerline (the same role $z$ plays above).

The original BIFROST-style sliced approach calculates

\[J_{\mathrm{total}}=\prod_i J_i,\]

with matrix order matching the order light encounters along the fiber. That approach is simple, but it is difficult to attach a meaningful error bound when linear birefringence, spinning, and other non-commuting terms vary along the path.

The Julia implementation instead assembles a local generator as a sum over mechanisms (all axes expressed in the transported frame):

\[K(s,\omega)=K_{\mathrm{bend}}+K_{\mathrm{twist}}+K_{\mathrm{ellipticity}} +K_{\mathrm{tension}}.\]

The bending contribution comes from path curvature κ(s), which is also how the cross-section layer's bending birefringence response is parameterized (bend_curvature_per_m): in the simplest stress model the magnitude scales like κ(s)^2, and its axis is the curvature direction projected onto the frame (curvature_in_bishop_frame). Since the curvature vector is transverse to the tangent, its two in-frame components satisfy kx^2 + ky^2 = κ^2, so the κ^2 in the magnitude cancels the one normalizing that axis and the generator is formed directly from (kx, ky) — polynomial, and exactly zero where the path is straight. Tension shares the bend axis but scales like κ(s)·F, one power of curvature, so it keeps an explicit magnitude. The intrinsic contribution's axes co-rotate with the material: spin_phase(path, s) + twist_phase(path, s). Twist additionally contributes photoelastic circular birefringence. Geometric torsion appears in none of these — see the frame page.

Each retardance is signed, not a bare magnitude: positive linear Δβ puts the slow axis at the generator angle, and bend/tension come out negative for silica. The sign conventions are stated once, in Bifrost.FiberPath.

The same decomposition exists for the frequency derivative:

\[K_\omega(s,\omega) =K_{\mathrm{bend},\omega}+K_{\mathrm{twist},\omega} +K_{\mathrm{ellipticity},\omega}+K_{\mathrm{tension},\omega}.\]

That keeps ordinary Jones propagation and DGD sensitivity propagation aligned: both use the same Fiber, wavelength, breakpoint partition, and adaptive integration strategy.

Numerical Propagation

The step is a fourth-order commutator-free exponential (exp_cf4_step):

\[J_{n+1}=\exp\!\left(h(\alpha_1K_1+\alpha_2K_2)\right) \exp\!\left(h(\alpha_2K_1+\alpha_1K_2)\right)J_n, \qquad K_i=K(s_n+c_ih),\]

with Gauss–Legendre nodes $c_{1,2}=\tfrac12\mp\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6}$ and weights $\alpha_{1,2}=\tfrac14\mp\tfrac{\sqrt3}{6}$. It is useful here because the solution of a constant-coefficient matrix ODE is exactly an exponential: for constant $K$ the two factors are $\exp(hK/2)$ each and the step is exact. Step by step, the method preserves the multiplicative structure of Jones propagation. Under the lossless assumption, after removing common phase, the Jones matrices live in SU(2).

The adaptive controller uses step doubling:

  • take one full step of size h,
  • take two half steps of size h/2,
  • compare the two results using phase_insensitive_error,
  • accept or reject the step,
  • update h with a fifth-root controller because the estimate scales as O(h^5).

Path breakpoints come from the built path:

fiber_breakpoints(fiber) = breakpoints(fiber.path)

Those breakpoints are the path's segment boundaries; a spin run starts at one, so it adds none of its own. propagate_fiber integrates each smooth interval with propagate_interval! in the fiber's material frame: with $\theta$ the accumulated spin and twist angle and $R(\theta)$ the transverse rotation, $J=R(\theta)\tilde J$ gives $d\tilde J/ds=\bigl(R^{\mathsf T}KR-\theta'\,G\bigr)\tilde J$ with G = [0 -1; 1 0]. The core ellipticity, whose axis turns with $\theta$, is constant there on a straight, so the step does not resolve its oscillation (src/fiber/material-frame.jl).

On a helix with a constant spin rate only the bend and tension terms vary in that frame: their axis turns at $\Omega=\tau_g-\theta'$, with $\tau_g$ the geometric torsion, so $\tilde K$ repeats with period $P=\pi/|\Omega|$. An interval holding two periods or more is integrated over one period, that propagator (with its $\omega$ sensitivity) is raised to the power of the whole periods, and the remainder is integrated (src/fiber/fiber-propagate.jl). An ensemble takes this shortcut only where every lane shares the period, as lanes strained alike by :T_K do.

The 2x2 Jones exponential uses a closed form based on Cayley-Hamilton. The implementation also factors out small numerical trace drift before applying the traceless formula:

\[\exp(A)=\exp(\operatorname{tr}(A)/2) \left[\cosh(\mu)I+\operatorname{sinhc}(\mu)\tilde A\right], \qquad \mu^2=-\det(\tilde A).\]

Here sinhc(mu) = sinh(mu) / mu, with a Taylor branch near zero. The full derivation is given in the appendix.

Uncertain segment lengths

When length-changing meta (:T_K, :tension) makes segment lengths MCM Particles, every lane builds its own geometry and the ensemble solve maps each lane's segments onto one shared grid. See The solve workflow, Stages 1–3.

DGD Sensitivity Propagation

The finite-difference DGD estimate used by the legacy implementation has the form

\[\partial_\omega J \approx \frac{J(\omega+\Delta\omega)-J(\omega)}{\Delta\omega}.\]

The Julia propagator instead integrates the sensitivity matrix G = partial_omega J directly:

\[\frac{dJ}{ds}=KJ,\qquad J(s_0)=I,\]

\[\frac{dG}{ds}=K_\omega J+KG,\qquad G(s_0)=0.\]

At the output, the PMD generator is

\[H_{\mathrm{PMD}}=-iJ^{-1}G.\]

output_dgd(J, G) returns the eigenvalue spread of that generator. For 2x2 MCM-valued matrices, output_dgd_2x2(J, G) computes the same spread using a closed-form Hermitian 2x2 formula, avoiding LinearAlgebra.eigvals.

The coupled sensitivity step is implemented using a closed-form Frechet derivative of the 2x2 exponential:

\[\exp\!\left(h\begin{bmatrix}K & K_\omega\\0 & K\end{bmatrix}\right) = \begin{bmatrix}E & F\\0 & E\end{bmatrix}.\]

This is implemented by exp_block_upper_triangular_2x2. Avoiding generic 4x4 LinearAlgebra.exp is important for MCM compatibility and is also faster for ordinary Float64 cases.

Appendix: Cayley-Hamilton 2x2 Exponential

For any 2x2 matrix A, Cayley-Hamilton gives

\[A^2-\operatorname{tr}(A)A+\det(A)I=0.\]

If A is traceless, then

\[A^2=-\det(A)I.\]

Define mu^2 = -det(A). Then

\[A^2=\mu^2 I,\qquad A^3=\mu^2 A,\qquad A^4=\mu^4 I,\ldots\]

The exponential series splits into even and odd powers:

\[e^A =\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{2k}}{(2k)!} +\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{A^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}.\]

Using A^(2k) = mu^(2k) I and A^(2k+1) = mu^(2k) A:

\[e^A = \left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu^{2k}}{(2k)!}\right)I + \left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\mu^{2k}}{(2k+1)!}\right)A.\]

Therefore

\[e^A=\cosh(\mu)I+\frac{\sinh(\mu)}{\mu}A, \qquad \mu^2=-\det(A).\]

At mu = 0, interpret sinh(mu) / mu -> 1, so e^A = I + A.
