The transverse frame and the birefringence gauge

The frame convention in which BIFROST expresses polarization is the parallel-transported (Bishop) frame. It fixes the meaning of every angle that enters the generators, explains why geometric torsion appears nowhere in the optics, and states the anchor convention and why the frame is continuous along the whole path. It is the companion to the implementation in src/geometry/path-geometry.jl (frame transport) and src/fiber/fiber-path.jl (generator assembly).

1. The propagation equation and what "gauge" means here

BIFROST integrates the Jones equation along the fiber arc length $s$:

\[\frac{dJ(s)}{ds} = K(s)\,J(s), \qquad J(0) = \mathbb{1},\]

with $K$ a traceless anti-Hermitian-times-$i$ generator (lossless propagation; see Deriving the generators). The two components of the Jones vector are the field amplitudes on some pair of transverse unit vectors $(e_1(s), e_2(s))$ with $e_2 = \hat{T}\times e_1$. That pair is a gauge choice: nothing physical depends on it, but every axis angle inside $K$ — the bend axis, the ellipse axis — is measured in it, so the equations are only as trustworthy as the frame convention is consistent. The solver (src/path-integral.jl) never sees the frame; the gauge lives entirely in how fiber-path.jl builds $K(s)$ from geometry queries.

2. Free propagation parallel-transports the field

For a transparent, locally isotropic medium guiding a transverse wave along a gently curved path (radii large compared to the wavelength and the cladding, which is the regime BIFROST is restricted to), Maxwell's equations force the electric field to stay transverse while exchanging direction with the tangent only at the minimal rate required by $E \perp \hat{T}$:

\[\frac{d\vec{E}}{ds} = -\left(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{E}\right)\hat{T}, \qquad \vec{k} \equiv \frac{d\hat{T}}{ds},\]

where $\vec{k}(s)$ is the curvature vector (magnitude $\kappa$, direction toward the local center of curvature). The field never rotates about the tangent. This is Rytov's law, observed in fiber as the Berry-phase rotation of polarization along helical paths (Tomita and Chiao, doi:10.1103/PhysRevLett.57.937; Berry, doi:10.1038/326277a0). The glass itself obeys the same rule: an unspun, untwisted cross-section is carried along the path without rotating about the tangent, so its frozen-in anisotropy axes are parallel-transported too (Ulrich and Simon, doi:10.1364/AO.18.002241).

3. The Bishop (relatively-parallel) frame

Define the transverse pair by the same transport law (Bishop, doi:10.2307/2319846):

\[\frac{de_1}{ds} = -\left(\vec{k}\cdot e_1\right)\hat{T}, \qquad e_2 = \hat{T}\times e_1 .\]

Properties that matter here:

  • Existence and smoothness everywhere. The transport needs only $\hat T(s)$; it is defined on straights, through inflections, and across joints where the curvature direction jumps. (The Frenet–Serret normal $\hat N = \vec k/\kappa$ needs $\kappa \neq 0$ and flips at inflections.)
  • Zero twist: $\langle de_1/ds,\, e_2\rangle = 0$ by construction — the defining property, enforced as a T-PHYSICS test in test/test_bishop_frame.jl.
  • Gauge freedom is one constant angle. If $(e_1, e_2)$ is relatively parallel, so is the pair rotated by any constant angle about $\hat T$. The frame is unique once $e_1(0)$ is chosen (Section 6).
  • Relation to Frenet–Serret. Where the FS frame exists, $e_1 = \hat N\cos\theta - \hat B\sin\theta$ with $\theta(s) = \theta(0) + \int_0^s \tau_{\mathrm{geom}}\,ds'$: the FS pair rotates about the tangent at the geometric-torsion rate relative to parallel transport. This identity is the closed-form helix transport in _parallel_transport_local(::HelixSegment, …).

4. Why the generator contains no geometric-torsion term

Expand the field on the transported pair, $\vec E = a_1 e_1 + a_2 e_2$. Substituting the transport law for both $\vec E$ (Section 2) and $(e_1, e_2)$ (Section 3) cancels the tangential exchange terms identically, leaving

\[\frac{d}{ds}\begin{pmatrix}a_1\\ a_2\end{pmatrix} = K_{\mathrm{aniso}}(s) \begin{pmatrix}a_1\\ a_2\end{pmatrix},\]

where $K_{\mathrm{aniso}}$ contains only material anisotropy. Free propagation — straight, bent, or helical — contributes nothing. Had we expanded on the FS pair instead, the relative rotation of Section 3 would inject a connection term $\tau_{\mathrm{geom}}\,(i\sigma_2)$ that must then be carried, spliced, and (as issues #88/#62/#24 showed) mis-carried across joints, inflections, and near-straight regions. In the transported gauge $\tau_{\mathrm{geom}}$ exits the optics entirely; it survives only as the shape diagnostic geometric_torsion/total_torsion, with no optical role.

5. The birefringence sources in this gauge

With the gauge fixed, optical generators come only from material anisotropy. A scalar phase such as spin_phase or twist_phase may rotate the axes used by a linear-retarder generator, but it is not itself a birefringence generator. The two are kept in separate tables below to make the taxonomy explicit.

Sign convention

The generators' forms and signs are stated once, in the FiberPath module docstring:

Bifrost.FiberPath — Module
FiberPath

Fiber assembly: binds an authored path to a cross section and assembles the local Jones generators K(s) and Kω(s) of dJ/ds = K(s) J.

Sign conventions

This is the single statement of the fiber generators' sign conventions. The time sign and the circular handedness they rest on are those of Bifrost.PolarizationMath: e^{−iωt}, so propagation multiplies an amplitude by e^{+iβs}; handedness judged looking toward the source.

  • Jones basis. The first component lies along the transported Bishop leg e1(s), the second along e2(s). An axis angle φ is measured from e1 toward e2.

  • Linear birefringence is signed, Δβ ≡ β(axis at φ) − β(axis at φ + π/2), so Δβ > 0 makes the axis at φ the slow one. Its generator (linear_birefringence_generator) is

    K_ℓ = (iΔβ/2) [cos 2φ   sin 2φ
               sin 2φ  −cos 2φ]

    and advances the axis at φ with phase +Δβ/2 per unit length.

  • Circular birefringence is signed, Δβc ≡ β_LC − β_RC. Its generator (circular_birefringence_generator) is the real rotation generator

    K_c = (Δβc/2) [ 0  1
               −1  0]

    whose left-circular eigenstate [1, i] advances with phase +Δβc/2 per unit length. A linear state's azimuth (from e1 toward e2) evolves as ψ(s) = ψ(0) − Δβc·s/2.

Enforced by test/test_birefringence_signs.jl.

spin_phase is not a birefringence source: it is an authored material rotation phase that advects the intrinsic axes through ellipticity_generator_K's axis argument. Mechanical twist appears twice for different reasons — the accumulated $\phi_{\mathrm{twist}}$ advects those same axes (the geometric rotation), while the local rate $\tau_m$ separately creates the photoelastic circular birefringence through photoelastic_response_to_twist_generator_K. The geometric rotation is carried only by the advected axes, never duplicated in the circular term.

With $\Delta\beta_c = \beta_{LC} - \beta_{RC}$ as defined there, a right-handed twist gives $\Delta\beta_c < 0$ — l-rotatory activity whose polarization rotation follows the twist, the sign measured and verified by Ulrich and Simon (doi:10.1364/AO.18.002241, $g = -n^2 p_{44} \simeq +0.16$).

The differential Jones (N) matrix of a twisted fiber is sometimes written as three additive terms — linear birefringence at $0°$, linear birefringence at $45°$, and circular birefringence (Tentori and Garcia-Weidner, doi:10.1364/OE.21.031725, Eq. 6). That is the same physics in a fixed frame: the two linear terms are the $(\cos 2\varphi,\ \sin 2\varphi)$ decomposition of a single linear/elliptical retarder whose axis rotates with twist, which here is carried by $\phi_{\mathrm{twist}}$ advecting _intrinsic_axis_in_bishop_frame, while the circular term is photoelastic_response_to_twist_generator_K. So the rotating-axis retarder plus circular generator already represents that three-term structure.

Implementation notes:

  • \[\vec k\cdot e_{1,2}\]

    are curvature_in_bishop_frame (fiber-path.jl): the projection of curvature_vector(path, s) onto bishop_e1/bishop_e2. The double-angle form $(\cos 2\theta_b, \sin 2\theta_b) = ((k_x^2-k_y^2)/k^2,\ 2k_xk_y/k^2)$ is normalization- and branch-free (_bend_axis_in_bishop_frame).
  • Bend-stress magnitudes follow Ulrich, Rashleigh, and Eickhoff (doi:10.1364/OL.5.000273); the spun-bent formulation parallels Przhiyalkovsky et al. (doi:10.1109/JLT.2020.3017795).
  • A bend also produces a polarization-common index shift (β_shift_stress_bend). The conformally transformed index profile of a bent fiber, $n(x)(1 + x/R_{\mathrm{eff}})$ with effective bend radius $R_{\mathrm{eff}} = R/(1+k)$ and $k = -(n^2/2)[p_{12}-\nu(p_{11}+p_{12})]$ (Kiiveri et al., doi:10.1117/1.OE.61.12.126106), displaces the mode toward the higher-index outer side and raises both polarizations equally. Being isotropic it stays out of the traceless generator $K$ and enters only the common-mode phase, leaving the differential phase and DGD untouched.
  • Both material phases are integrals of authored rates and are continuous along the whole path by construction, across segment joints and spin-run boundaries alike.

Two axes, one frame

Two helpers in fiber-path.jl deliver a linear-retarder axis in the Bishop frame, and they are not two routes to the same number — they orient two physically distinct birefringence sources that merely share the frame.

  • _bend_axis_in_bishop_frame orients the bend/tension axis. It is purely geometric: curvature_in_bishop_frame projects $\vec k = d\hat T/ds$ onto $(e_1, e_2)$, and the double-angle pair follows from $(k_x, k_y)$ without ever forming the angle itself (next subsection). It is blind to spin and twist, and its direction is undefined where $\kappa = 0$.
  • _intrinsic_axis_in_bishop_frame orients the ellipticity axis. It rides on top of the frame: a frozen ellipse angle measured from $e_1$ plus the accumulated material rotation $\phi_{\mathrm{spin}} + \phi_{\mathrm{twist}}$. It is always defined and is independent of the path's curvature direction.

Both return the same (cos 2φ, sin 2φ) double-angle pair consumed by linear_birefringence_generator; the source they belong to lives in the name, not the type.

The bend axis: the double angle, and the $\kappa^2$ that cancels

Why the bend axis is delivered as a ratio of quadratics rather than an angle, and why the bend term needs no $\kappa \neq 0$ guard while the tension term does (issue #315).

The curvature vector is transverse, exactly. Since $\hat T$ is a unit vector, $\hat T\cdot\hat T = 1$, so $2\,\hat T\cdot d\hat T/ds = 0$ and $\vec k \perp \hat T$ identically — not to leading order. So $\vec k$ lies wholly in the span of $(e_1, e_2)$ and its projection loses nothing:

\[k_x = \vec k\cdot e_1, \quad k_y = \vec k\cdot e_2, \qquad k_x^2 + k_y^2 = \lVert\vec k\rVert^2 = \kappa^2 .\]

Writing $\vec k = \kappa(\cos\varphi\, e_1 + \sin\varphi\, e_2)$, the pair is just polar coordinates in the transverse plane: $(k_x, k_y) = \kappa(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$. That pair is what _bend_axis_vector_at returns (and what curvature_in_bishop_frame returns on the per-$s$ side). Rotating the frame by a constant $\delta$ about $\hat T$ sends $\varphi \to \varphi - \delta$.

Why a double angle. A retarder eigen-axis is a line, not an arrow: $\varphi$ and $\varphi + \pi$ are the same physical state, so any coordinate for it must be $\pi$-periodic — which $(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$ is not. Equivalently, $K_\ell$ is a traceless symmetric rank-2 object, so a frame rotation by $\varphi$ rotates its components by $2\varphi$; on the Poincaré sphere linear birefringence sits on the $S_1$–$S_2$ equator and a real-space rotation by $\varphi$ is a rotation by $2\varphi$ about $S_3$. The factor of two is the spin-2 nature of the retarder, not a convention. One visible consequence: $\vec k \to -\vec k$ leaves the pair below unchanged, since both entries are even in $(k_x, k_y)$ — pinned as a T-PHYSICS test in test/test_fiber_path_generators.jl.

The pair without trigonometry. Applying the double-angle identities to $(k_x, k_y) = \kappa(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)$,

\[\cos 2\varphi = \frac{k_x^2 - k_y^2}{k_x^2 + k_y^2}, \qquad \sin 2\varphi = \frac{2 k_x k_y}{k_x^2 + k_y^2},\]

which is _bend_axis_at (and _bend_axis_in_bishop_frame). Preferred over $\operatorname{atan2}$ plus $\cos/\sin$ on three counts: no transcendentals; no branch cut, so it is smooth wherever $\kappa \neq 0$; and conditional-free, which MCM requires — atan2 branches on argument signs and errors on Particles.

What happens at $\kappa = 0$. Both expressions become $0/0$. That is honest rather than a numerical wart: a straight segment has no bend plane, so the direction of curvature genuinely does not exist. Note that the frame is perfectly well defined there (Section 3) — only the angle measured in it is not.

What does have a limit is the product with the magnitude, and for bending the two singular factors are the same one. Bend stress gives $\Delta\beta_b = C\kappa^2$ (Section 5), so

\[\Delta\beta_b\cos 2\varphi = C\kappa^2\,\frac{k_x^2-k_y^2}{\kappa^2} = C\,(k_x^2 - k_y^2), \qquad \Delta\beta_b\sin 2\varphi = C\,2k_xk_y .\]

Polynomial in $(k_x, k_y)$: no division, finite everywhere, and exactly zero on a straight segment. The $\kappa^2$ that makes the magnitude vanish is the same $\kappa^2$ that makes the direction diverge; the physics was never singular, only the intermediate factorization into magnitude $\times$ direction. This is why the generator consumes _bend_axis_vector_at and the bend term carries no $\kappa \neq 0$ guard. The cancellation crosses a layer boundary — the magnitude is a cross-section quantity and the normalization a geometry one — so the cross-section layer exposes $C$ on its own as _birefringence_bending_coefficient.

Axial tension is the instructive contrast. It shares the bend eigen-axis but scales as $\Delta\beta_t \propto \kappa F$ — one power of curvature — so only half the $\kappa^2$ cancels and a $\lvert\kappa\rvert = \sqrt{k_x^2+k_y^2}$ survives. Its slope is unbounded at zero, and that kink is real: tension birefringence is linear in curvature magnitude. So the tension term keeps a $\kappa = 0$ boundary guard, where the bend term needs none.

6. The anchor $e_1(0)$ and continuity

Parallel transport determines the frame up to one constant: $e_1(0)$. BIFROST uses a static lab-frame rule (_initial_frame_from_tangent): take the world axis least aligned with the launch tangent and Gram–Schmidt it,

\[e_1(0) = \frac{\hat a - (\hat a\cdot\hat T)\hat T}{\lVert \cdots \rVert}, \qquad \hat a = \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{\hat x,\hat y,\hat z}\ |\hat T\cdot \hat a|.\]

For the common $\hat T(0) = \hat z$ launch this is simply $e_1(0) = \hat x$. The rule is deliberately blind to curvature: it depends only on the launch direction, is reproducible by hand, never moves when downstream geometry is edited, and has no special cases.

From the anchor, build places the whole path in one loop and advances $e_1$ through every segment — connectors included — by that segment's closed-form parallel transport (_parallel_transport_local), so each segment's entry $e_1$ is its predecessor's exit $e_1$. The frame is therefore continuous along the whole path by construction (a T-GUARDRAIL test in test/test_bishop_frame.jl, issue #89).
